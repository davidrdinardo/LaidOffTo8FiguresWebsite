3 Reasons It's Easier to Build a Business in Canada Than the U.S.

WATCH ON YOUTUBE → LISTEN ON SPOTIFY →
01 IN THIS EPISODE

David DiNardo makes the case that Canadian small business owners hold three structural advantages over founders in the U.S. and U.K.: a 12 percent small business corporate tax rate, universal healthcare that keeps group benefit costs far lower, and a much smaller share of the population starting businesses. Drawing on 15 years running his accounting company, he explains how retaining profit inside a corporation let him buy real estate every year for a decade and grow to a team of 40, and when a sole proprietor should incorporate.

02 KEY TAKEAWAYS
  1. Canada's small business corporate rate of about 12 percent compares with personal rates that can reach 54 percent, a spread of roughly 40 points that stays in the business if you keep growing it rather than paying yourself out.
  2. David's rule of thumb: stay a sole proprietor until roughly $100,000 in revenue, then incorporate, because the lawyer, accountant, banker and extra filings only pay off past that threshold.
  3. Passive investments like real estate and stocks bought inside the corporation are taxed at a higher rate than active business income, so reinvesting in the business keeps you at the low rate.
  4. For 10 years David tracked net income and projected tax throughout the year, then split each year's after-tax cash between one real estate purchase and growing the firm, which reached 40 people and millions in revenue.
  5. David's numbers: his group benefit plan cost $200 to $300 per employee in Canada, versus $2,000 to $3,000 per employee in the U.S., freeing cash to hire or expand.
  6. David's numbers: one in 20 Canadians start a business versus one in 10 in the U.S. and one in 12 in the U.K., which he says roughly halves your competition and is why his early accounting practice had more demand than it could serve.
