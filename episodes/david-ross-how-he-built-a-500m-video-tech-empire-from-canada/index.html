01 IN THIS EPISODE

David Ross, CEO of Ross Video, tells David DiNardo how he joined his father's near-bankrupt Ontario switcher company at 24, stopped a sale that was one signature away, and grew it into a 1,500-employee, $500M broadcast technology giant with 17% annual growth for 35 straight years and no outside capital. He explains the acquisition strategy behind 24 deals, why Ross Video became an employee-owned company, and why he thinks Canada is the best place to build a hardware and software business.

02 KEY TAKEAWAYS
  1. Ross Video has grown 17% a year for 35 consecutive years with no down years and no external capital, doubling roughly every five years; David's stated goal is to double again to $1B in revenue.
  2. The key to the company's success, in David's words, is hiring really smart people and not pissing them off, so innovators build inside the company instead of leaving to start their own.
  3. Losing a university sale to Grass Valley because he could not compare the two products taught him that knowing your competition matters as much as knowing your product; he then read every competitor manual end to end.
  4. Rather than buying direct competitors and merging overlapping product lines, Ross buys small companies with the next big technology; one sub-$1M software acquisition now generates over $100M a year.
  5. Ross Video's ESOP values shares by a fixed formula (0.8 times revenue plus 3 times profit) and has about 700 employee shareholders, roughly one in every two employees.
  6. David's leadership team meets every Wednesday from noon to 6 p.m. and works through about 20 issues a week, with an average of two and a half working days from issue to decision.
  7. By David's math, hiring in Canada costs about half of hiring in the US once R&D credits, grants and the exchange rate are factored in, and free trade agreements make Canadian input costs lower than an American company's.
  8. The best place to raise capital is your customers: sell a product with a good margin and repeat, and avoid early venture terms that cost you control of the company later.
