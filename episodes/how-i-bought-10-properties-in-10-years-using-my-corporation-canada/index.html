How I Bought 10 Properties in 10 Years Using My Corporation (Canada)

WATCH ON YOUTUBE → LISTEN ON SPOTIFY →
01 IN THIS EPISODE

David DiNardo explains the playbook he used to buy one rental property a year for ten years after being let go from his corporate job at 24. He funded the down payments with retained earnings from his Canadian operating company, which is taxed at roughly 12 percent instead of personal rates of up to 54 percent, and placed each property in its own holding company with its own bookkeeping file. The system gave him liability protection, monthly visibility into every property's numbers, and a structure that scaled cleanly as the portfolio grew.

02 KEY TAKEAWAYS
  1. Fund real estate from corporate cash flow rather than salary: a Canadian small business keeps about $88,000 of every $100,000 earned, while the same income taken personally can lose 35 percent or more to tax before it reaches the down payment.
  2. Only buy a property if the rent covers the mortgage and all expenses with a reserve left over for capital expenditures like a roof or furnace, regardless of whether you put 5, 20, or 30 percent down.
  3. Weigh the opportunity cost of the down payment against other uses: stocks return roughly 6 to 8 percent, real estate 10 to 14 percent, and a growing business can return 100 to 300 percent.
  4. Keep rental properties out of your operating company and in separate holding companies so a lawsuit against the business cannot reach the real estate.
  5. Set up a separate accounting file per holding company so you get monthly financials for each property and can hand a bank organized statements instantly when refinancing.
  6. Passive rental income inside a corporation is taxed at about 50 percent versus 12 percent for active business income, so monitor net profit monthly and make needed capital purchases before year end to manage the tax bill.
  7. If you skip separate corporations, at minimum give each property its own checking account; monthly variance tracking is how David caught a running toilet that pushed one water bill from $100 to $500.
