From Construction Worker to CEO: Shawn Watts on How an Injury Built Corfix

WATCH ON YOUTUBE → LISTEN ON SPOTIFY →
01 IN THIS EPISODE

Shawn Watts spent ten years as a unionized ironworker in Ottawa before the flood of daily safety paperwork on job sites pushed him to build Corfix, a health-and-safety compliance and time-tracking platform for specialty trades contractors that now has 40 employees and customers across Canada and more than 20 US states. He tells David DiNardo how he went from zero salary in year one to a Series A, why a wasted first $150,000 build taught him to hire a technical lead in-house, and how he treats ADHD as the trait that lets him thrive in chaos. The conversation covers L-SPARK, raising from family, customers and government grants, and why the best time to raise money is when you don't need it.

02 KEY TAKEAWAYS
  1. Corfix started because job-site paperwork went from one document a week to three a day; Shawn saw crews faking a week of forms every Friday and knew a digital system could fix both the burden and the liability.
  2. Shawn made $0 his first year at Corfix after leaving a six-figure ironworker job with a full pension, paid himself $60,000 in year two, and only passed his old income in year three.
  3. The first $150,000 from a family friend was largely wasted on a basement developer and then an agency build, because Shawn could not yet specify what to build; a young in-house developer on $60,000 plus options replaced the entire agency.
  4. Corfix bootstrapped roughly $3 million through grants, small friends-and-family rounds of $600,000 to $700,000 a year and a customer round before raising a Series A from a US investor.
  5. Shawn's fundraising lesson: once he stopped chasing VCs and focused on execution, inbound investors chased him, and pitching with 'a dollar in equals three out' beats 'I need to pay my staff'.
  6. The ideal Corfix customer is a specialty trades contractor with 50 to 500 workers in a regulated commercial market such as Ontario, New Jersey, Massachusetts or New York.
  7. Shawn's most expensive mistake was expanding into new markets too quickly by hiring local reps without the support infrastructure at home; his lightning-round non-negotiable is working-at-heights safety because falls kill more workers than anything else.
